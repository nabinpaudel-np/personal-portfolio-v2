In any project where more than three people are moving work forward, the most expensive question is the same one: *who actually owns this?* Not who is helping. Not who is in the loop. Who, when the deadline slips or the deliverable breaks, is the one person whose name goes on the post-mortem?

That is the question the **RACI Matrix** was built to answer.

## What RACI stands for

RACI is an acronym for four roles you assign to every task, deliverable, or decision in a project:

- **R — Responsible.** The person doing the work. There can be multiple R's on a task.
- **A — Accountable.** The single person who answers for the outcome. There is exactly **one A** per task. Not two. Not "the team." One name.
- **C — Consulted.** Subject-matter experts whose input shapes the work before it's done. Two-way communication.
- **I — Informed.** Stakeholders who need to know what happened but don't shape the work. One-way communication.

The magic isn't the letters. It's the act of writing them down, in a table, before the work starts.

## How to build one without overthinking it

You don't need a tool. A spreadsheet is fine.

1. List the major deliverables or decisions down the left column. Aim for 8–15 rows. More than that and you're documenting theater.
2. List every person with skin in the game across the top.
3. For each cell, write **one** letter. R, A, C, or I. Never two letters in one cell.
4. After the first pass, scan the rows and ask: does every row have exactly one A? Does every row have at least one R? If not, fix it before you share.

That's it. Two hours of work up front saves weeks of "I thought you were handling that."

## When RACI helps

RACI is built for projects where the work crosses functional boundaries — engineering + design + marketing + sales, or four vendors on a single integration. It does three things well:

- **Clarity in chaos.** When a task has no A, it has no owner. When it has three A's, it has a fight.
- **Empowerment for the R.** Engineers and designers stop waiting for permission when they know exactly which decisions they own.
- **Faster communication paths.** C and I tell everyone in the project who's a stakeholder and who's just watching.

It is not a Lean tool, an Agile ceremony, or a replacement for good judgment. It is a forcing function to make role conversations explicit before the work begins.

## Common RACI mistakes

Most RACI matrices fail for the same reasons:

- **Too many A's per row.** If a deliverable has three accountable owners, none of them are accountable. Pick one.
- **Confusing R and A.** The team member executing the work is the R. Their manager — or the PM, or the project sponsor — is the A. These are different people.
- **C overload.** If every stakeholder is C on every row, you've created a notification chain, not a decision structure. Be ruthless with C.
- **Treating it as static.** RACI lives in a living document. Update it when scope changes, when people rotate off, when decisions get reassigned.

## RACI vs. RASCI

Some teams add an **S — Supportive** role for people who help the R but aren't consulted on direction. This works for long-running operational projects where handoffs matter. For most product and engineering work, plain RACI is enough. Don't reach for RASCI until plain RACI is already running cleanly in your team.

## A worked example

Imagine a feature launch with four roles: PM, Tech Lead, Designer, Marketing Lead.

| Deliverable                | PM | Tech Lead | Designer | Marketing |
| -------------------------- | -- | --------- | -------- | --------- |
| Scope document             | A  | C         | C        | I         |
| Visual design              | I  | C         | A/R      | I         |
| API implementation         | I  | A/R       | C        | -         |
| Launch announcement copy   | C  | I         | I        | A/R       |
| Post-launch metrics review | A  | R         | I        | R         |

Notice: every row has exactly one A. The PM appears on most rows because they are accountable for the whole feature — but they are only the *doer* (R) on the metrics review.

## Who creates the matrix

The project manager, working with the team leads. The PM is the right person because they see all the moving parts, but every A on the matrix should be a person who actually owns that work — not the PM inserting themselves into every row.

When in doubt: the person whose calendar clears when that deliverable slips is the A.

## Bottom line

RACI is a four-letter answer to a recurring problem. It does not need a certification, a software license, or a workshop. It needs a table, a list of names, and the discipline to keep it updated for the life of the project.

If your team is currently arguing about who owns what, you don't need a new framework. You need a RACI, written down, shared, and defended.
